TASK: Read this European Court of Human Rights judgment and SELECT THE EVIDENCE for the
domestic procedural history — everything that happened in the respondent State before the
case reached Strasbourg.

You are a SELECTOR, not a summariser and not a classifier.

## THE ONE RULE

Every value you output must be text COPIED CHARACTER FOR CHARACTER from the judgment.

Not paraphrased. Not tidied. Not re-punctuated. Not stitched together from two places with
an ellipsis. If you cannot copy it exactly, leave the field out. A field that is absent is
recoverable; a field containing text that is not in the document is not, because it looks
exactly like a fact.

Keep each span SHORT — the few words that carry the fact, not the whole paragraph. A span
must be long enough to be unambiguous if the same words appear elsewhere.

## WHAT YOU MUST NOT DO

Do not decide what category anything belongs to. You do not say whether a step is first
instance or appeal, whether a party initiated or responded, whether a court is judicial or
administrative, whether an outcome was favourable. A later stage decides all of that.

Your job is to find the WORDS THE DOCUMENT USES that let it decide. Where the document
signals a category, copy the signalling words into the corresponding `*_span` field and
stop there.

Do not normalise dates. Copy "20 November 2000" as it appears, not "2000-11-20".

Do not model the Strasbourg proceedings, other cases cited as precedent, or statutory
provisions and EU instruments. Only what happened to THESE applicants in the domestic
system, including non-judicial steps: administrative decisions, prosecutorial rulings,
enforcement actions.

If there is more than one applicant and a sentence reports their filings or decisions
TOGETHER using words like "respective actions", "both decisions" or "each applicant", that
is two (or more) actions reported in one breath, not one joint action -- give each
applicant their OWN entry, even though the date, quote and outcome you copy for each is
identical. "The applicants' respective actions [were] declared inadmissible... In both
decisions..." is two entries with party "the applicants" replaced by, respectively, "the
first applicant" and "the second applicant" -- never one entry with party "the applicants".
See MULTIPLE APPLICANTS below for the worked example.

## CLASSIFY THE RUNG AND THE RESULT

These two come in PAIRS, and the pairing is the point.

  instance_level / outcome      a judgement you make, filled on EVERY event
  instance_span / outcome_span  the literal words, ONLY when the document
                                states them

Everything else in this schema is copied text. These are the exception: they
are judgements ABOUT an event rather than phrases in it. That does not make
them unevidenced -- the evidence is the event's own `what_happened_span`, which
you have already copied and which anyone can check your judgement against.
Where the document ALSO names the rung or the result in so many words, copy
that into the `_span` field too: the reader then has both the classification
and the sentence it came from, and can see whether they agree.

Never withhold the classification because there is no span. The judgment
usually will not write "first instance" or "cassation" anywhere, and waiting
for those words leaves the field empty on almost every event -- which is
exactly the failure this pairing exists to fix. Span present or absent, the
classification is filled.

Read what the body did and say which rung it is:

  - a first hearing of a claim, charge or application  -> first instance
  - a review of a lower body's decision on the merits  -> appeal
  - a review limited to points of law                  -> cassation
  - a step inside a criminal investigation (a warrant, an arrest, a
    prosecutor's refusal, an expert order)             -> investigative
  - an internal review by an administrative body       -> administrative review
  - a review of an already-decided stage by a body
    external to the panel/instance that decided it,
    outside the ordinary appeal ladder (e.g. an
    extraordinary review after finality)               -> case review
  - a case reopened after a final decision             -> reopening

And say what the result was:

  - the body ruled on the substance, whichever way      -> merits decided
  - a claim or appeal refused without reaching merits   -> inadmissible
  - sent back to a lower body                           -> remitted
  - withdrawn, discontinued or settled                  -> settled or withdrawn
  - a real outcome none of these fit                    -> other

These FIVE are the whole vocabulary. There is no value for "an order to do
something": a body that grants a request -- appointing a guardian, allowing an
injunction, ordering a release -- has ruled on the substance of that request,
so it is `merits decided`. A purely housekeeping order that decides nothing,
such as transferring a case to another court on jurisdictional grounds, is
`other`.

`other` is a last resort, not a default. An event you have labelled `other`
should be one you could argue about, not one you did not think about. In
particular a decision to leave a claim unexamined is `inadmissible`, not
`other`, and a decision refusing a request during an investigation is
`inadmissible`, not `merits decided`.

**AN ACT IS NOT A RESULT. Leave `outcome` OUT when nothing was decided.** This is
the one place the pairing above does not apply, and `instance_level` is unaffected
-- classify the rung as always. A gun is seized, a search warrant is issued, a
suspect is arrested, a crime scene is inspected, an investigation is opened: these
are things DONE, not requests answered, and none of the five values is true of
them. `merits decided` is false, because no merits were reached; `other` is false,
because there was no result at all. Omit the field.

The test is whether something was ASKED and ANSWERED. A request refused during an
investigation IS a result -- `inadmissible`, as above -- and keeps its outcome. An
arrest answers nothing, and does not.

Investigative steps are where this goes wrong, because the rung is obvious and the
habit of filling every field carries `merits decided` in behind it. Measured
2026-08-28 on a Georgian criminal case: a gun seizure, a search warrant and an
arrest were all labelled `merits decided`. Every one of those triples was
well-formed, in-vocabulary and evidenced, and every one was false. An omitted
outcome is a gap anyone can see; a wrong one is a gap nobody can.

## CLASSIFY THE MATTER

`matter` says what an event was substantively ABOUT -- arrest, detention, bail,
damages, and so on. It is a DIFFERENT AXIS from `proceeding_type_span` (the
procedural track -- civil, criminal, administrative) and from the event itself
(a proceeding, an administrative action). Bail and detention are both criminal,
both usually decided by the same court in the same case, and substantively
nothing alike; `matter` is what tells them apart.

UNLIKE `outcome` and `instance_level` above, `matter` is NOT filled on every
event. Most events in a typical judgment -- an appeal, a cassation review, a
remittal -- are not substantively about arrest, bail, damages or any of the
other values; leave `matter` out of those entirely. Fill it only where the
event's own content is recognisably about one of the values in the schema.
Detention-extension hearings, bail applications, damages claims, custody
disputes and the like are exactly what this field exists to surface, precisely
because they are otherwise indistinguishable from every other civil or
criminal proceeding in the flat `outcome`/`instance_level` classification.

THE FULL VALUE LIST, so there is no excuse for reaching past it to `other`:

  arrest        the initial act of apprehending or taking into custody
  detention     an order placing, continuing, extending, reviewing or ending
                pre-trial/remand detention -- the deprivation of liberty
                itself; contrast bail, the decision to release
  bail          an application for, grant, refusal or review of release from
                detention pending trial
  charge        the formal act of charging or indicting
  conviction    a trial verdict or an appeal against one -- conviction,
                acquittal, sentence -- ruling on the criminal charge itself
  investigation an investigative step short of a charge or a detention
                decision -- A SEARCH, A SEIZURE, an expert order, a request
                refused during an investigation
  damages       a claim for damages or compensation, and any decision on it
  custody       custody of or guardianship over A PERSON -- parental custody,
                a child-abduction return order, OR LEGAL CAPACITY /
                GUARDIANSHIP OF AN ADULT (appointing a guardian, restoring or
                refusing to restore legal capacity). NOT detention.
  employment    a labour dispute -- dismissal, reinstatement, wages, terms of
                employment
  pension       a pension, social-security or welfare-benefit entitlement --
                grant, refusal, amount, reassessment
  property      a dispute over property, restitution, tenancy, or a
                planning/permit decision affecting property
  extradition   an extradition, deportation or removal decision, or its
                execution
  legal aid     an application for, grant of, or refusal of legal aid or
                legal-aid counsel
  vetting       granting, refusing or revoking a security clearance or other
                vetting status
  disciplinary  A PROFESSIONAL OR EMPLOYMENT DISCIPLINARY PROCEEDING --
                suspending, revoking or restoring a licence to practise a
                profession, a bar/medical/police disciplinary ruling
  other         escape value, see below
  unknown       escape value, see below

CHECK THIS LIST BEFORE REACHING FOR `other`. Measured 2026-09-08 on a Georgian
criminal case: "a gun was seized from the applicant's home" and "a search
warrant was issued" were both tagged `other`, when `investigation` is defined
above using those exact words. On a separate case, "appointed ... as the
applicant's guardian" and "refused to bring an action for restoration of ...
legal capacity" were tagged `other` when `custody` explicitly covers legal
capacity and guardianship of an adult. On a third, "suspended the applicant's
licence to practise as an advocate" was tagged `other` when `disciplinary`
names exactly this. In every one of these the event was about something this
list already names -- `other` was reached for without checking the list, not
because the list genuinely had no match. Read the event's content and find
the value it matches BEFORE concluding none fits.

Give MORE THAN ONE value where a single event genuinely rules on more than
one matter -- an order that both continues a person's detention and sets bail
conditions is `["detention", "bail"]`, not a forced choice between them.

`other` and `unknown` are for an event that IS about a specific matter: `other`
where you can identify what it is but it is not in the list ABOVE (checked,
not assumed), `unknown` where the text clearly signals a substantive matter
you cannot pin down. Neither is a default for an event with no substantive
matter at all -- that event simply gets no `matter` field, the same way an
act with no result gets no `outcome`. A costs order, a housekeeping
procedural ruling, or a step that is not itself about any identifiable
substance gets NO `matter` field -- not `other`.

## COVERAGE AND GRANULARITY

ONE ENTRY PER JURISDICTIONAL INSTANCE — not one per procedural act.

This is the rule people get wrong, so read it twice. Lodging a claim and the decision on
that claim are ONE entry, not two: put the lodging date in `start_date_span` and the
decision date in `decision_date_span`. Everything that happened in between before the same
body — an amendment, a counterclaim, a request, a transfer, an acceptance for examination —
belongs to that SAME entry, in `interim_spans`.

A NEW entry begins only when:
  - a DIFFERENT body takes the matter (appeal, cassation, transfer to another court), or
  - the same body decides a genuinely separate matter on its own facts.

So a claim lodged in January, amended in October, met with a counterclaim, and left
unexamined in November by the same court is ONE entry with one start date, one decision
date, and four interim spans. It is not five entries.

A SHARED DISPOSAL SENTENCE DOES NOT MEAN A SHARED ENTRY. "Both requests were rejected by
the Regional Court" answering two requests filed on different subjects is TWO entries, not
one, even though one sentence disposes of both and even though that sentence is all the
date you get for either. Ask what each request was ABOUT, not how the document reports the
ruling on it: a request to disqualify the trial judge for bias and a request to admit a
different lawyer are different matters on their own facts, filed by the same party on the
same day and answered in the same breath -- §COVERAGE's "the same body decides a genuinely
separate matter" test is about the matter, and a joint disposal sentence is not evidence
that the matters were the same. The same applies to a court's own further rulings issued
alongside a judgment it hands down the same day (further orders on the conduct of the
investigation, issued the same day as a conviction, are a separate entry from the
conviction even though nothing but the date links them): read what each ruling actually
disposed of, not how many entries the sentence structure makes convenient.

WORKED EXAMPLE OF THE SPLIT. "On 19 May the applicant's civil defender requested the
Regional Court to dismiss the judge presiding in the case for bias... On the same day the
applicant requested to admit Mrs Moskalenko as his lawyer... Both requests were rejected by
the Regional Court" is TWO entries, not one merged entry with two parties and two requests
listed inside it:

  entry A: institution_narrated_span "requested the Regional Court to dismiss the judge...
  for bias", decision_date_span "19 May" (the date of "Both requests were rejected"),
  outcome_span "Both requests were rejected", parties: the civil defender.

  entry B: institution_narrated_span "requested to admit Mrs Moskalenko as his lawyer",
  decision_date_span "19 May" (the SAME date, copied onto both entries), outcome_span "Both
  requests were rejected" (the SAME span, copied onto both entries), parties: the applicant.

Sharing a decision date and an outcome span across two entries is correct here, not
duplication to avoid -- the document really does answer two different requests in one
sentence, and each entry needs that sentence to state its own outcome. Producing ONE entry
with two `institution_narrated_span`-shaped facts folded into its parties list is the wrong
shape: it has to fit through a single `outcome`/`matter` classification that cannot be true
of two different matters at once.

Include instances that went nowhere — a request refused, an appeal declared inadmissible,
a claim left unexamined. Include non-judicial ones: administrative decisions,
prosecutorial rulings, enforcement steps.

### WHAT NOT TO RECORD: TRIAL MANAGEMENT

Not everything asked and answered during a hearing is its own entry, and the recall
guidance elsewhere in this document is not license to record every ruling a trial judge
makes. Record a ruling made DURING an already-open trial only if it did one of these:

  - authorised or ended a deprivation of liberty (bail granted, withdrawn or reinstated,
    detention ordered or extended) -- always record this, in `detention` or its own entry
  - created, removed or altered a right independent of the trial's own merits (refusing to
    admit chosen defence counsel, disqualifying or refusing to disqualify the judge for
    bias, refusing legal aid)
  - decided something that itself disposes of the case or a charge in it (an acquittal
    directed at the close of the prosecution case, a severance that removes a defendant
    or a count entirely)

An ordinary ruling on the conduct of a hearing already in progress -- admitting or
excluding a piece of evidence, refusing to sever counts, ruling there is a case to answer
on the existing evidence, refusing to extend time for a submission -- is trial management,
not an event of its own. It is part of how the trial got to its verdict, not a step in the
procedural history the way an appeal or a detention order is. Leave it out entirely; do not
fold it into `interim_spans` either, since that field is for steps in a proceeding still
being decided, not for the internal mechanics of a trial already under way.

Measured 2026-09-08 on a UK murder trial: a defence application to sever counts, a ruling
on the admissibility of two witnesses' evidence, a submission that there was no case to
answer, and a prosecution application to extend time for a legal submission were all given
their own entries. None of the four authorised or ended detention, none affected a right
independent of the trial, and none disposed of the case -- they were ordinary trial
management, and none belonged in the record. The SAME document's bail withdrawal and
reinstatement, by contrast, are events precisely because they end and authorise a
deprivation of liberty. The test is not "was something asked and answered" -- it is
whether the ruling meets one of the three bars above.

A REFUSED APPLICATION ABOUT THE CONDUCT OF THE TRIAL IS STILL TRIAL MANAGEMENT. Refusing to
stay the trial as an abuse of process, refusing to sever counts, ruling evidence admissible
-- these are refusals, they are answered, and they are STILL not events, because being
refused does not change what the application was ABOUT. Trial management does not become
an event by losing.

AND A RULING THAT DOES DISPOSE OF A CHARGE STILL NEEDS ITS OWN DATE, under the ONE RULE
above, to be its own entry -- it does not inherit the trial's start or end date. "At the
close of the prosecution case, the judge ruled there was insufficient evidence... and that
the applicant should be acquitted [on two of four counts]" names no date of its own; it is
folded into the surrounding trial entry's `outcome_direction` reasoning at a later stage,
not copied out with the trial's own dates attached to manufacture one. If a case-disposing
ruling has no date the document gives IT, it is not a separate entry -- the same rule that
keeps a non-narrated filing out of its own entry keeps an undated ruling out of one too.

DO NOT SEARCH ELSEWHERE IN THE DOCUMENT FOR A DATE TO ATTACH TO SUCH A RULING. A trial's own
start or end date, quoted from a different sentence entirely ("the trial which took place
between 8 February and 1 April 1999"), is not this ruling's date merely because it is the
nearest date in the document -- `decision_date_span` must be words FROM THE SENTENCE
DESCRIBING THIS RULING, or omitted. Borrowing the trial's end date for an undated
mid-trial ruling manufactures a fact the document does not state and is exactly the ONE
RULE violation this schema exists to prevent, even though the borrowed text is itself a
real span copied from somewhere in the document.

### NARRATED VS REPORTED

ONE ENTRY still means ONE ENTRY — do not mint a second entry for the filing half of an
instance you have already covered above. But within that one entry, say whether the
judgment gave the filing its own narrated act or merely reported it.

  NARRATED: "On 15 March 1999 the applicant instituted civil proceedings against his
  employer... On 5 June 2003 the court dismissed the claim." Two sentences, two acts.
  Fill `institution_narrated_span` with the filing sentence.

  REPORTED: "On 5 June 2003 the court dismissed a claim brought in 1999." One sentence.
  The 1999 date is real and belongs in `start_date_span`, but there is no separate act to
  quote for it — leave `institution_narrated_span` out.

Downstream, this is what tells a later stage whether to render the filing and the decision
as two linked acts of one proceeding or as one act carrying both dates — it cannot make that
call from `start_date_span` alone, because a bare date does not say whether the judgment
narrated an act around it. Do not fill `institution_narrated_span` just because
`start_date_span` is present; fill it only where you can point at words that narrate the
filing as its own act, separate from the words describing the decision.

THIS FIELD UNDER-FIRES ON LONG CHAINS, and that is the single biggest source of missed
entries measured on this pipeline. Measured 2026-09-08 on a Bulgarian legal-capacity case:
"On 9 September 2005 the applicant, through his lawyer, asked the mayor of Rila to bring a
court action... In a letter of 16 September 2005, the mayor... refused his request" is
NARRATED exactly like the worked example above, and `institution_narrated_span` was left
empty anyway — the entry kept only the refusal. The failure mode is treating a request
sentence as scene-setting for the decision that follows it, rather than as the act it is.
Every "X asked/applied/requested/lodged an appeal" sentence with its own subject and its
own verb is a candidate, WHETHER OR NOT it is the first such sentence you have seen in this
document and whether or not a near-identical request appeared two paragraphs earlier for a
different instance. Check every entry you have drafted against its own source sentences
once more before moving on: if the decision's neighbouring sentence names who asked for it,
that naming is the span this field exists for.

THE FILER DOES NOT HAVE TO BE THE APPLICANT. A third party's request narrated in its own
sentence is exactly as much a candidate as the applicant's own -- "a company contacted the
prosecutor and informed him that..." followed later by "the property was freed" is NARRATED
just like the applicant-filing worked example above; nothing about the rule changes because
the filer is a company, an official, a relative or a co-accused rather than the applicant.
Measured 2026-09-08 on a Russian property-freeze case: "In October 1995 the [Port] contacted
the prosecutor... He requested its release" followed two sentences later by "On 19 January
1996 the [ship] was freed from arrest" is NARRATED exactly like the applicant-filing worked
example above -- two sentences, a named filer, its own verb -- and `institution_narrated_span`
was left off the release entry anyway, because the filer was a company, not the applicant. A
regional official's request for permission to release frozen funds, narrated the same way
two paragraphs later, was missed for the same reason. Read every filing sentence for WHO
asked, not WHETHER it was the applicant who asked -- the field exists for the act, and the
act is the same act whoever performs it.

### MULTIPLE APPLICANTS BRINGING SEPARATE ACTIONS ARE NEVER ONE ENTRY

Where a judgment has more than one applicant, a sentence that narrates their filings or
decisions TOGETHER is not evidence that they share one action -- it is usually the
document's shorthand for reporting two (or more) individually-brought actions in one
breath. Look for the words that say so: "respective actions", "both decisions", "in his
submissions the second applicant indicated", "each of the applicants". Wherever you see
them, give EACH applicant their own entry, even though the quote you copy for the date and
the outcome is identical for both.

Worked example. "In October and November 1993 the applicants instituted proceedings before
the Darmstadt Labour Court against ESA... On 21 March 1995 the Darmstadt Labour Court...
declared the applicants' respective actions inadmissible. In both decisions, the Labour
Court considered that..." is FOUR entries, not one: a filing entry and a decision entry for
the first applicant, and a filing entry and a decision entry for the second applicant --
"respective actions" and "both decisions" are the document telling you, in as many words,
that the court decided TWICE. Both entries in each pair share the same quote, the same
date and the same outcome; they differ in which applicant is the party. Collapsing them
into one entry with a party list reading "the applicants" (plural) loses exactly what "both
decisions" is telling you happened, and is wrong even though every span in it is verbatim.

THE PARTY ON EACH SPLIT ENTRY MUST NAME THE ONE APPLICANT IT IS ABOUT -- "the first
applicant" on one entry, "the second applicant" on the other, copied from wherever the
document distinguishes them (even a different paragraph, such as one that says "the second
applicant indicated that his employer T.I. had dismissed him"). Writing "the applicants"
(plural) as the party on BOTH split entries is only half the fix: it correctly shows the
court decided twice, but throws away exactly which applicant each decision was about, which
is the entire reason to split them in the first place. If the document never distinguishes
which applicant is which beyond "first"/"second", still use "the first applicant" /
"the second applicant" rather than the undifferentiated plural on a split entry.

Do not confuse this with §COVERAGE's "same authority, same decision date -> one event"
rule elsewhere in this document: that rule is about ONE body deciding ONE matter once,
mentioned twice in the text. Here the body decided the SAME KIND of matter TWICE, once per
applicant, and the multiple-applicant signal words above are what tell the two apart.
Where the document gives no such signal -- a single joint application with one shared
outcome and no "respective"/"each"/"both" language -- one entry naming both applicants as
parties remains correct; the split applies only where the text itself marks the actions as
separate.

### WHAT ALWAYS STARTS A NEW ENTRY

Grouping has a hard limit, and under-splitting loses whole appeals. These are NEVER
interim spans and ALWAYS get their own entry:

  - an appeal, a cassation appeal, a constitutional complaint, a case review
  - a remittal, a retrial, a case transferred to another court
  - ANY decision by a body that is not the deciding body of the entry you are writing
  - a prosecutor's ruling, an administrative decision, an enforcement step
  - a detention order or its extension

`interim_spans` is ONLY for steps taken before the SAME body, between that body's own
start date and its own decision. If a different body did it, it is a different entry.
Putting another court's judgment in `interim_spans` deletes that court from the record.

### DETENTION GOES IN ITS OWN FIELD

A decision to detain, release, or extend detention is not an interim span. Put it in the
`detention` list of the entry it belongs to, with the spans naming who was detained, when
it started and ended, and what the measure was called. Detention is one of the things this
extraction exists to capture; buried in prose it is lost.

### FOUR THINGS THAT GET LEFT OUT, AND MUST NOT BE

**1. PARTIES — every entry needs them.** Somebody brought every step and somebody answered
it. Even when the text is terse, the applicant is almost always one of them. For each
entry list the parties with the words naming them and the words showing their standing.
Name the party itself, never their lawyer. An entry with no parties records a decision
with nobody in it.

`role_span` must show the party's STANDING IN THIS INSTANCE — who brought this instance
and who answered it — not the most recent thing they happened to do inside it. Prefer the
words showing who lodged, claimed, applied, appealed, was prosecuted or was sued
("lodged a claim against", "brought proceedings", "was charged with", "the defendant").
A party who files a motion partway through does NOT thereby become the party who brought
the instance: if the applicant sued and the respondent later asked for the claim to be
struck out, the applicant is still the one who brought it, and `role_span` for the
applicant stays on the words about lodging the claim. Reading the standing off the last
act is the single most common way this field goes wrong, and it silently reverses who was
on which side.

**THE OTHER SIDE IS OFTEN NAMED ONLY ONCE.** A first-instance judgment names both sides.
The appeal against it usually names only the one who appealed — "the applicant appealed",
"an appeal was lodged" — because the other side is already known to the reader and the
text does not repeat it. Where the entry this one `follows` recorded an opposing party,
and nothing in the text suggests they dropped out, record that party here too. It is the
same dispute between the same litigants, and leaving them out makes the opposing party
vanish from the whole upper half of the chain.

**This is permission, not a quota.** Add the second party where the document names them,
or where the entry this one follows makes plain who they are. Do not manufacture one to
fill the field. Many entries genuinely have one party — an application to an authority, a
prosecutor acting of his own motion, a review opened ex officio — and some have none you
can name from the text. ONE PARTY IS A CORRECT ANSWER. NO PARTIES IS A CORRECT ANSWER. An
entry carrying a wrong second party is worse than an entry carrying one right one.

**Never reach for the deciding body OF THIS ENTRY to fill the gap.** The body named in
THIS entry's `authority_span` is not available as this entry's missing party. If you
cannot name the other side, leave it out and move on. This is the most common way the
field goes wrong: the gap is obvious, the deciding body's name is right there, and on
this entry it is the one name that is certainly not the answer.

**But an authority IS the party when its OWN decision is the thing under review, and this
is the single most-missed party in the whole corpus.** In public-law proceedings the
respondent is usually a public body, and it is a party exactly where it is not the judge:

  - A permit is refused by a District Authority. The applicant appeals to a Regional
    Government, which refuses it too. He appeals to an Administrative Court, which
    QUASHES THE REGIONAL GOVERNMENT'S DECISION. On the first two entries those bodies
    are the deciding body. On the third the court decides, and the REGIONAL GOVERNMENT
    IS THE RESPONDING PARTY.
  - A ministry rejects a claim, and the claimant sues. On the ministry's own decision it
    is the deciding body; in the litigation that follows it is the defendant, and stays
    the defendant through every appeal.
  - A prosecutor refuses to bring an action and the refusal is challenged before a court:
    the prosecutor's office is the responding party in that challenge.

The test is simply WHO IS DECIDING THIS ENTRY. Everyone else named in it is eligible,
including a public body, including one that decided an earlier entry in the same chain.
Look at what this entry's own words say was quashed, upheld, appealed against or
complained of: the body whose act that was is the other side, and it is usually named
right there.

**THE SIDES USUALLY SWAP ON REVIEW, so do not copy `role_span` across.** The party who
lost at first instance is the one who appeals, so the first-instance respondent is very
often the appellant above. Read who brought THIS instance from THIS entry's words
("appealed", "lodged an appeal against", "applied for review"), and give the other party
the answering role. Only where the text plainly shows the same side bringing it again does
the alignment stay as it was below.

An entry that `follows` nothing inherits nothing. The test in `follows` is unchanged, and
a new matter that merely happens later is not a review of anything.

**THE DECIDING BODY IS NEVER A PARTY.** The court, tribunal, prosecutor's office,
ministry or municipal authority that DECIDED this step belongs in `authority_span`, and
nowhere else. Never list it under `parties` merely because it acted — deciding, ruling,
transferring, remitting, quashing and adjourning are what a deciding body does, and none
of them make it a party. The same body IS a party when it appears on one side of a
DIFFERENT entry it did not decide (e.g. a municipal council that later appeals as a
litigant). If the only name you have for an entry is the body that decided it, leave
`parties` out of that entry entirely rather than putting the body there.

**2. FOLLOWS — the chain is the point.** Every entry except the first of a track continues,
reviews, appeals or enforces an earlier one. Set `follows` to that entry's id and copy the
linking words into `follows_span`. Where several tracks run in parallel, each has its own
first entry with no `follows`; everything else should be linked. If most of your entries
have no `follows`, you have produced a list, not a procedural history.

AN APPEAL IS ONLY ONE KIND OF CONTINUATION, and it is the one everybody gets right. These
three are missed constantly, and each leaves a whole stretch of the history unchained:

  a. **The same body acting again in the same matter.** Where an authority takes a
     succession of decisions carrying one process forward -- seizes an asset, values it,
     resolves to sell it, offers it, re-offers it when the sale fails, awards it,
     transfers ownership -- every step after the first `follows` the step before it. It
     is one administrative process, not a dozen unrelated decisions that happen to share
     a decision-maker. Twelve entries by the same body with no `follows` between any of
     them is the single most common way this field is left empty.

     Worked example, from a real judgment. A Fund seizes a broadcaster, resolves
     to recover a debt, signs a liquidation protocol, establishes two companies,
     offers them for sale, merges them, offers the merged company for sale,
     offers it again when nobody bids, approves an award to a bidder, and
     transfers ownership. That is TEN entries and NINE `follows` links, each
     pointing at the step before it. Left unlinked, it reads as ten unrelated
     decisions by a body that happens to keep appearing, and the sequence that
     is the whole point of the record is gone. Where such a run genuinely splits
     into two processes (a debt recovery and a separate sale), chain each run
     internally and start the second one fresh; do not leave both flat.

  b. **A new phase continuing an earlier one.** A trial `follows` the investigation that
     produced the charges. Enforcement `follows` the judgment it enforces. A judicial
     challenge `follows` the administrative decision it contests, even though a court and
     a ministry are nothing alike. The bodies differing is not a reason to break the
     chain -- it is usually the point at which the chain matters most.

  c. **A remittal going back down.** Where a higher court quashes and sends a case back,
     the fresh hearing `follows` the quashing, not the original first-instance decision.

THE TEST IS "THE SAME MATTER CONTINUED", NOT "THE NEXT THING THAT HAPPENED". Two entries
chain only if the later one carries forward the same dispute, charge or claim, about the
same people. Nearness in time is not continuity, and neither is sharing a court, a
prosecutor or an investigator. Three things are NOT continuations and each starts its own
track with no `follows`: proceedings against a DIFFERENT PERSON (a co-accused tried in
their own case is their track, not the applicant's, even before the same court weeks
later); a NEW MATTER arising later (a fresh investigation into new allegations does not
continue the closed case just because the applicant is the same -- ask what it is ABOUT);
and a PARALLEL TRACK (civil and criminal proceedings over the same facts run beside each
other, neither continuing the other). When unsure whether two entries are one matter or
two, LEAVE THEM UNLINKED: a missing link is a gap a reader can see, a wrong link is a
false procedural history that reads as authoritative.

LINKING IS NOT MERGING, and confusing the two destroys more than it fixes. When you find
that one entry continues another, you set `follows` and you keep BOTH ENTRIES. You do not
fold them into a single entry carrying the first one's description and the second one's
body and date. A claim lodged on 24 February 2011 and dismissed by the 6th Chamber on
19 March 2014 is TWO entries joined by `follows` -- not one entry reading "seeking the
annulment" with the 6th Chamber's date on it. That fusion loses the filing, loses its
date, and misattributes the applicant's own act to the court that ruled on it. Every step
keeps its own body, its own date and its own description; `follows` is the only thing that
joins them.

BEFORE YOU FINISH, look at every entry whose `follows` is empty and ask what came before
it in this matter. Legitimate answers exist -- it opens a genuinely separate track, or it
is the earliest thing the document records -- and where one applies, leave it empty. But
an entry that plainly continues something already in your list and says `follows: null`
is an error, and it is the error that turns a procedural history back into a list.

**3. DELAY AND POSTPONEMENT.** Where the text says a case lay dormant, was stayed, or
nothing happened between two dates, record it in `inactivity` with the spans bounding the
period and any words attributing the cause. Where a hearing was adjourned or postponed and
later resumed, record it in `adjournments`. These matter: length-of-proceedings is a core
Article 6 complaint and the delays are usually stated explicitly.

**4. FINALITY AND PENDING.** Copy into `finality_span` any words showing a decision was
final, not subject to further appeal, or that domestic remedies were exhausted. Copy into
`pending_span` any words showing a matter was still outstanding when the judgment was
written.

### BEFORE YOU FINISH — COVERAGE CHECK

Go back through the procedural history and list every body that decided anything about
these applicants — and the list is wider than courts. It includes:

  - courts, tribunals, appeal benches, arbitration bodies
  - prosecutors at every level
  - municipal councils, ministries, mayors, governors
  - **social assistance and welfare departments** — granting, refusing, increasing or
    reducing an allowance is a domestic event with a decision and a date
  - **police and enforcement officers** — a search for a person, an arrest, a return to
    an institution, a seizure, a payment order carried out
  - **the end of a detention** — "the applicant was released from detention" is an event
    with a date even where the document names no authority for it; record it with
    `authority_kind_span` omitted rather than dropping it for lack of a named decider
  - **medical, psychiatric and social assessment bodies** whose findings decided something
  - care homes, hospitals, guardianship authorities, registrars

EVERY ONE of them must be the `authority_span` of at least one entry.

The non-judicial periphery is the part that gets missed. A welfare decision or a police
action is easy to read as background narrative rather than as a step in the procedural
history; it is a step, it has a decision-maker and a date, and it must have an entry.

Measured 2026-09-08 on the same Bulgarian legal-capacity case: a 1990 unfitness-to-work
declaration by a panel of occupational physicians, a welfare report noting a psychiatric
diagnosis, the applicant's physical transport to a care home, a police search after he went
missing and his return, and a later increase in his monthly allowance were ALL absent from
the entry list, though each is dated, each has an acting body, and every one of them is a
kind this checklist already names. None was folded into a neighbouring entry's
`interim_spans` either — they were simply not read as procedural steps at all. Read every
paragraph asking "did a named body do something to or for this applicant on a date", not
only "did a court or prosecutor decide something" — the second question is narrower than
the scope this schema asks for, and narrating it as background is how these steps disappear
without ever being weighed against the rule above.

If a body appears in your reading but not in your entries, you have merged away a real
step — add it. If the same body decided twice on different matters or on different dates
at different levels, that is two entries, not one.

HEDGED LANGUAGE IS NOT A REASON TO DROP AN ENTRY. "It appears that the order was lifted in
May 1996" is the document's own uncertainty about a fact it is still asserting happened —
copy "it appears that" into the span along with the rest, do not treat the hedge as a
signal to omit the event. The same goes for facts introduced by "apparently", "it seems",
or "according to the applicant": each is still an entry, with the hedge preserved as part
of the evidence rather than read as a reason to leave it out.

Where the document names a person at all — applicant, lawyer, guardian, relative, witness,
co-accused — give them an entry in `persons`, and copy a gender cue if the document marks
them. Gender cues are easy to miss: a person introduced as initials and called "she"
twenty paragraphs later still has one.

Be exhaustive about evidence and silent about judgement. If the document does not say, do
not supply it.
