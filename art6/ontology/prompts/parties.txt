## WHAT A PARTY IS

The parties are the people or bodies whose dispute the entry decided: the one who
brought it, and the one who answered it.

**THE BODY DECIDING THAT ENTRY IS NEVER ONE OF ITS PARTIES.** Each entry names the body
that decided it. That body is the judge of that entry, not a side in it. Do not return it.

**BUT A PUBLIC AUTHORITY IS VERY OFTEN THE OTHER SIDE.** This is the party most commonly
missed, so look for it specifically. When a court reviews what an authority decided, the
authority is the responding party in that review — it decided the earlier entry, and it
is a litigant in this one. Read the entry's own words: whatever was quashed, upheld,
appealed against, complained of or challenged, the body whose act that was is the other
side, and it is usually named right there.

Worked example. A District Authority refuses a permit; the applicant appeals to a
Regional Government, which refuses too; he appeals to an Administrative Court, which
quashes the Regional Government's decision.
  - on the first entry the District Authority DECIDES; the party is the applicant
  - on the second the Regional Government DECIDES; the party is the applicant
  - on the third the court decides, and the parties are THE APPLICANT and THE REGIONAL
    GOVERNMENT

The same goes for a ministry that rejected a claim and is then sued, a prosecutor whose
refusal is challenged before a court, and a State body defending a compensation claim.

**THE SIDES USUALLY SWAP ON REVIEW.** The one who lost below is the one who appeals. Read
who brought THIS entry from THIS entry's words, and give the other party the answering
role.

## HONESTY

One party is a correct answer, and no parties is a correct answer. Many entries genuinely
have one side — an application to an authority, a prosecutor acting of his own motion, a
review opened on the body's own initiative. Do not invent a second party to fill a slot,
and never use the deciding body to fill one. AN ENTRY WITH A WRONG SECOND PARTY IS WORSE
THAN AN ENTRY WITH ONE RIGHT ONE.

Name the party itself, never their lawyer.
